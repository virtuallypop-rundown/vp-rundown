Artist development

SXSW 2027 showcase funding: next deadline 27 November

PRS Foundation's International Showcase Fund has its second SXSW 2027 deadline on Friday 27 November 2026, with a final round on 6 January 2027. Grants of up to £8,250 are available to UK-based artists, bands, songwriters and producers who have a formal invitation to an industry-facing international showcase. Artists in the North of England who are invited to SXSW apply through the same fund under a new joint scheme with the Northern Music Export Office.

Why it matters: If an act you manage or develop has an SXSW invitation, this is the main UK route to help with flights, accommodation, visas and per diems. Applicants need the invitation in hand, and the guidance asks for confirmed industry meetings and a 25% contribution to costs, so start preparing now.

Sources: PRS Foundation deadlines · PRS Foundation International Showcase Fund · PRS Foundation and Northern Music Export Office

Artist development

Wide Days development programme closes 24 November

Wide Days, Scotland's music convention in Edinburgh on 29 and 30 April 2027, is taking applications for its 12-month Talent Development Programme until 11:59pm on Tuesday 24 November 2026. Four Scotland-based acts will get mentoring, sync preparation, one-to-one industry meetings, a grant and a showcase slot, with support from PRS Foundation and Creative Scotland. A separate New From Scotland showcase has a rolling deadline running to 26 February 2027.

Why it matters: This is only for Scotland-based acts, but it's worth passing on to any you work with. The New From Scotland slot is aimed at artists who already have a team and are building an audience across the UK.

Sources: Wide Days · Musicians' Union

Music marketing & social

Spotify lets every artist block fake releases from their profile

From 17 November, Spotify's Artist Profile Protection is available to all artists through Spotify for Artists, after a beta that began in March. Artists who opt in are alerted when new music is delivered under their name, and can remove themselves from releases that aren't theirs, whether they come from impersonation, AI fakes, metadata errors or another act with the same name. Removed releases won't appear on the profile, count towards its stats or be recommended to its listeners. Artists have up to 28 days after a release goes live to act, and a stricter pre-approval setting is being tested with a small group.

Why it matters: Anyone managing an artist profile should switch this on now, especially for acts with common names or similar-sounding names. It's a simple way to keep stray releases away from your audience and out of your stats.

Sources: Music Business Worldwide · TechCrunch

Live music & touring

UK music hits a record £8.4bn, but growth is slowing

UK Music's This Is Music 2026 report puts the industry's contribution to the economy at a record £8.4bn in 2025, up 5.7%. Exports reached £5bn and employment was broadly flat at 220,500. Big tours drove much of the growth, and music tourism spending rose to £11.2bn. Industry leaders warned that growth is slowing and uneven, with pressure still on grassroots venues and emerging artists. The report's policy asks include removing barriers to EU touring, business-rates relief for studios, no free pass for AI firms to use creators' work, and an earlier government consultation on digital replicas.

Why it matters: These figures will shape the lobbying around Friday's UK-EU summit and future touring support. The digital replicas consultation could also affect how a performer's voice and likeness are protected, which matters for tribute and heritage productions.

Sources: UK Music · Billboard · NME

Entertainment law

Producer sues over the beat behind Travis Scott's 'What to Do?'

Israeli producer Niv Kalisky, known as Early Yellow, has filed a lawsuit in federal court in New York against Travis Scott, Don Toliver and Sony Music over the 2019 track 'What to Do?'. He says he shared a beat with people working on the song, no licence was ever agreed, and he has received no royalties despite being credited on streaming services. He is asking the court to recognise a 50% share of the recording and to award damages. The defendants had not commented publicly at the time of reporting, and none of the claims has been tested.

Why it matters: Whatever the outcome, the case is a reminder of why producers and writers should get beat-sharing and splits agreed in writing before a track is released.

Sources: Rolling Stone India
